1What was asked, and what I built

The brief asks for an agent that creates mind maps from a context or requirement and goes beyond generic structures — researching alternatives, asking probing questions, challenging assumptions, and refining the map from the answers.

I built Probe, a browser agent that runs a four-step loop:

1 Brief  →  2 Clarify  →  3 Map  →  4 Refine
            ↑                          │
            └──────── the answers change the map ────┘
  1. Brief. You say what you are trying to work out. Purpose, audience and depth are optional.
  2. Clarify. Before mapping anything, the agent asks the 3–5 questions whose answers would most change the shape of the map, each with the default it will assume if you skip. Skipping is one click and always allowed.
  3. Map. A tree where every node carries why it is there, a confidence level, and a source — your input, model, or assumption. Every first-level branch also carries alternatives ("are these the only options?" answered concretely, each with its trade-off) and probes — questions to you that would change that branch.
  4. Refine. The agent puts its hardest open question at the bottom of the screen. Your one-line answer rebuilds the map, and the result is shown as a diff — added nodes edged green, reworded ones amber, with a written change note and a one-click undo.

The map is not read-only at any point: rename a node inline, add or delete children, lock a node so a regeneration cannot touch its wording, take an alternative framing in place of the branch it argues against, deepen a branch, or mark a branch useful or generic. Undo/redo covers all of it.

Two run modes, stated in the interface

ModeWhat it isWhere

This split is a constraint, not a design choice: a published artifact cannot call an LLM API. Rather than hide it, the app names the mode in the top bar, in the footer, and in every message where the limit bites — including refusing to pretend it can map an arbitrary brief in recorded mode.

2Users and needs

Primary user. Someone doing early, messy thinking work with a real deliverable attached — a UX researcher scoping a study, a founder planning a launch, an engineer planning twelve weeks of learning. They are competent in their domain. They do not need an AI to tell them what a mind map is; they need one that has read their brief and can tell them what they are missing.

Primary tasks.

  1. Turn a paragraph of intent into a structure I can argue with.
  2. Find the branch I would not have thought of, and the assumption I am resting on.
  3. Leave with something I can paste into a doc, a ticket, or a plan.

Do not rely on it for: facts, numbers, prices, citations, anything specific to your company it cannot know, or professional judgement. Both lists are printed on the first screen, before the user's first run — not buried in a help page.

4What works, and what needs improvement

Works

  • The clarifying step changes the output visibly. Answering "only completers are reachable" or "we stop at 200 orders a week" produces a map that could not have been written from the brief alone.
  • Skipping is genuinely cheap, and the assumptions it creates are visible on the map rather than hidden in the model's head.
  • The refinement diff is the feature that makes iteration feel safe: you can see the four nodes that moved, and undo them in one click.
  • Failure states are specific. Nothing in the app produces a blank screen or a bare "something went wrong".

Needs improvement

  1. One template branch per map (see the rubric scores above) — the biggest quality gap and the first v0.2 change.
  2. Confidence is compressed. Almost everything is high or medium; a scale that never says low carries no information.
  3. Recorded mode is a partial experience. One expansion and one refinement per case. Honest, but a reviewer without an API key sees a rehearsal, not the agent.
  4. No memory between sessions. Close the tab and the map is gone. Exports exist, but a "resume" would cost little.
  5. The probe dock shows one question at a time with no sense of how many remain or which matter most.
  6. Nothing yet learns from the 👍/👎 signals. They are captured in the session log and go nowhere. Week 3 should feed voted-down nodes back into the refine prompt.

7Lessons learnt so far

  1. The interesting design work was in the refusals. Deciding what the agent will not do — map a one-word brief, pretend recorded mode is live, hide a skipped question — did more for its credibility than any feature.
  2. A skip is a design decision, not a shortcut. Turning every skipped question into a visible assumption was the change that made the clarify step worth its friction.
  3. Iteration is only usable if it is reversible and visible. Before the diff and undo existed, refining felt like gambling with a map you had already invested in.
  4. The platform constraint shaped the product. No LLM call is possible from a published artifact, so the honest options were "make the limit visible" or "fake it". Naming it produced a better artifact — and, unexpectedly, a good demonstration of graceful failure.
  5. Design bugs hide in the gap between source and output. The dock squeezing the canvas to a fifth of the window, and small text failing contrast in the light theme, were both invisible in code and obvious in a screenshot. Looking at the running thing found what reading it could not.
